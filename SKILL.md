# Product Research

Find the product or offer that best satisfies the user's stated objective using current, comparable evidence. Preserve that objective throughout the research: do not silently turn “cheapest” into “cheapest good one”, “best” into “most popular”, or the user's requirements into personal preferences.

## Frame the decision

Understand the real use before looking for products. Extract:

- The jobs the product must do and the context in which it will be used.
- The primary objective: lowest upfront cost, lowest lifetime or unit cost, best performance, best value, lowest risk, or another stated target.
- Hard constraints: conditions that make an option ineligible, such as compatibility, maximum budget, location, or a firm delivery deadline.
- Preferences: attributes with an ideal range but acceptable trade-offs. Record a preferred range and tolerance where suitability changes gradually rather than inventing a sharp cutoff.
- Secondary criteria, their relative importance, and the facts still unknown that could change the winner.

Ask one targeted question only when plausible answers would materially change the result and cannot be inferred safely. Otherwise proceed with a stated assumption. Treat a product supplied by the user as a candidate to verify, not as evidence to dismiss or accept uncritically.

Set research depth in proportion to the purchase. A commodity consumable usually needs broad price and compatibility checking; an expensive, durable, safety-sensitive, or business-critical product needs stronger performance, reliability, support, ownership-cost, and safety evidence.

## Build a defensible candidate set

1. Resolve the exact product identity. Record the manufacturer, model or part number, variant, pack size, condition, region, included components, and any compatibility standard that affects eligibility.
2. For a system or bundle, define component compatibility and evaluate the complete setup. Do not infer system quality from one strong component or overlook separates when they are allowed.
3. Separate the **product** from the **offer**. The same item can have different sellers, prices, delivery dates, return terms, bundles, or subscription conditions.
4. Map the source classes that materially cover the relevant market. Depending on the category, these may include major marketplaces, large retailers, specialist sellers, manufacturer-direct stores, comparison engines, local stockists, or used/refurbished channels when allowed.
5. Search broadly within those sources. Use exact identifiers, compatibility terms, pack sizes, common synonyms, retailer categories, filters, pagination, and internal search. General web search is useful for discovery but is biased towards indexed and well-ranked products; do not let search-engine visibility define the candidate market.
6. Continue until additional relevant sources and query variants stop producing eligible candidates that could plausibly beat the current leaders, or until the evidence supports only a deliberately limited claim.

A market-wide superlative requires market-wide support. If the search is not exhaustive, say “lowest price I found” or “best of the products compared” and identify the material coverage rather than claiming “the cheapest available” or “the best”.

## Make offers and evidence comparable

Apply hard constraints before ranking. Compare like with like and preserve the distinction between lowest upfront price and lowest unit or lifetime cost.

For each serious contender, capture as relevant:

- Exact model, variant, condition, pack quantity, included components, and seller.
- The physical and functional attributes that affect the user's actual use, not only headline category labels.
- Current item price and delivered total, including shipping, taxes, handling, required accessories, and other unavoidable fees.
- Coupon, membership, trade-in, subscription, minimum-order, or multi-buy conditions.
- Unit cost or expected lifetime cost only when it matches the user's objective.
- Stock, location-specific delivery or collection date, order cutoff, return costs and window, warranty, and seller identity.
- The observation date and direct product or offer URL.

Do not let a larger pack win a lowest-upfront-cost request merely because its unit cost is lower. Do not compare a coupon, subscription, used item, or marketplace offer as if it had the same conditions as an ordinary new purchase.

Use each evidence type for what it can establish:

- Manufacturer documentation or a recognised standards source for official compatibility and specifications.
- The live retailer page and checkout state for offer-specific price, seller, stock, exact variant, delivery, and purchase terms.
- Independent tests that use disclosed, consistent methods for comparative performance.
- Long-term owner data or reliability surveys for durability and failure patterns.
- User reviews for recurring real-world issues and use cases, not as a direct measurement of product quality.

Check reviews across more than one source when review evidence could change the decision. Look beyond the mean star rating: consider review count, recency, distribution, whether variants are pooled, repeated failure themes, incentives, reviewer context, and whether the reviewed item matches the current model and seller. Do not invent a universal rating or review-count threshold.

For safety-sensitive categories, check the relevant official recall or safety-alert source for the user's market. Do not infer safety from marketplace availability, ratings, or the absence of complaints.

## Rank according to the brief

Set the decision rule before selecting the winner:

- **Cheapest:** lowest current delivered upfront cost among eligible offers.
- **Lowest unit or lifetime cost:** comparable cost over the stated quantity, usage, or ownership period, with assumptions shown.
- **Best performance:** user-relevant results compared under compatible test methods.
- **Best value:** user-relevant benefits against comparable total cost, with material weighting choices stated.
- **Shortlist:** distinct options that represent useful trade-offs, not arbitrary near-duplicates.

Keep quality, seller, warranty, and return evidence in its proper role. If the user asked for the cheapest compatible item, rank eligible items by delivered price and use quality evidence as a stated sanity check or caveat. Do not quietly promote a costlier product because it feels safer. Exclude a cheaper item only for a demonstrated hard-constraint failure, material safety issue, likely fraud or counterfeit, or another explicit deal-breaker; explain the exclusion. When risk is meaningful but not disqualifying, show the objective winner and a safer alternative with the trade-off made explicit.

Do not adopt a publisher's overall score when its weighting does not match the user's priorities; inspect the underlying results. Avoid false precision in weighted scoring when the inputs are weak. If two options are close or the winner depends on an uncertain assumption, vary that assumption or weighting and report what would make the recommendation change.

When the user's needs or constraints change, update the brief and rerank the candidate set. Do not preserve an earlier favourite through inertia.

## Verify the result

Before answering, open the leading offer and any close alternative. Confirm the exact variant, seller, delivered price, stock, order cutoff, and location-specific delivery or collection promise. Cross-check any compatibility or specification that determines eligibility against a primary or otherwise authoritative source.

Treat product-family pages, search snippets, shopping cards, and generic retailer delivery policies as discovery evidence only. Delivery can depend on the exact variant, seller, time, account, and address. If final checkout state is inaccessible, state exactly what is verified—such as “next-day service offered”—and what is not—such as arrival on a particular date to the user's address.

Resolve conflicting sources where possible; otherwise show the conflict and lower confidence. A successful page load or citation does not prove the claim is correct.

## Report the decision

Lead with the answer that follows from the user's objective. Include:

- Exact product and offer, seller, current delivered price, observation date, and direct link.
- Why it wins against the primary objective and use case.
- Any condition, assumption, or material risk that could change the decision.
- A compact comparison of only the alternatives that clarify a real trade-off.
- The search boundary and calibrated confidence, especially for superlative claims.

Distinguish verified facts, user-supplied claims, calculations, and judgement. Cite claims near the evidence that supports them. State when price, stock, review data, compatibility, or delivery could not be verified live. Never imply personal testing when relying on published tests or owner reports.
